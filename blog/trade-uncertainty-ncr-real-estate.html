How International Trade Uncertainty Affects NCR Commercial Real Estate

Long before a trade dispute shows up in a vacancy report, it shows up in a boardroom, as a decision to wait one more quarter before signing anything long-term.

TL;DR

Trade policy uncertainty does not usually move commercial real estate through dramatic vacancy swings. It moves it through hesitation — firms delaying long-term lease commitments until the picture clarifies. In the National Capital Region, that hesitation plays out differently depending on how exposed a given firm is to trade-sensitive sectors, and it tends to favour flexible space over long leases for the duration of the uncertainty.

A Market That Moves on Policy, Not Just Rates

Commercial real estate decisions are usually discussed in terms of interest rates, vacancy rates, and construction costs. Trade policy rarely makes that list, yet for a market like the National Capital Region — where a meaningful share of tenant demand is directly or indirectly tied to federal policy, trade-exposed industries, and the firms that advise them — international trade uncertainty is a real input into how much space gets leased, and on what terms.

The mechanism is not dramatic. Firms do not typically vacate space because of a trade dispute. What they do is hesitate: a firm considering a five-year lease renewal decides to sign for two years instead, or to hold off signing at all until the next round of negotiations or tariff decisions resolves. That hesitation is quiet, individually small, and collectively significant across a market where many tenants are making similar calculations at the same time.

The Government-Adjacent Buffer

The National Capital Region has a structural feature that most commercial markets do not: a large share of its economic activity is tied to the federal government itself, which does not contract in the same way trade-exposed sectors do when international conditions turn uncertain. Departments, agencies, and the consultancies, law firms, and advisory practices that serve them tend to see steadier demand through periods when export-oriented sectors elsewhere in the country are pulling back.

This does not make the region immune to trade uncertainty — it makes it a different kind of exposure. Firms whose work is directly trade-sensitive (customs brokers, cross-border logistics, sectors facing tariff exposure) feel the uncertainty first and most acutely. Firms whose work is adjacent to government policy — regulatory advisory, public affairs, procurement consulting — often see demand increase during periods of trade uncertainty, because uncertainty itself generates a need for advice on navigating it.

Uncertainty shows up as hesitation, not vacancy. The first sign is shorter lease terms and delayed decisions, well before any vacancy figure moves.

Exposure is not uniform. Trade-sensitive sectors pull back; government-adjacent advisory sectors often see steadier or increased demand.

Flexibility becomes valuable precisely when certainty is scarce. The premium on being able to adjust footprint quickly rises during uncertain periods.

What Uncertainty Does to Leasing Decisions

When a firm is unsure how the next twelve to twenty-four months will unfold, the rational response is to preserve optionality rather than lock in a long-term position. That shows up in a handful of consistent ways: shorter lease terms, more interest in space that can be given up or expanded on short notice, and more scrutiny on whether a given square footage commitment is actually necessary versus simply customary.

For a landlord or a market overall, this produces a period where demand for traditional long-term leases softens even as demand for flexible arrangements — virtual offices, coworking, short-term licences, and pay-per-use meeting space — holds up or increases. The total amount of business activity requiring a presence in the region has not necessarily declined; what has changed is the form firms want that presence to take while the underlying uncertainty persists.

Why Flexibility Becomes the Hedge

A firm facing genuine uncertainty about its near-term trajectory has, in practice, three options: commit to a long lease and accept the risk that circumstances change before the term is up; forgo a physical presence entirely and accept the credibility cost that comes with it, particularly for firms doing business with government or regulated clients where a professional address still matters; or maintain a flexible arrangement that provides the address, the meeting space, and the professional infrastructure without the multi-year commitment.

The third option has become more attractive as trade conditions have grown less predictable, not because flexible space is inherently better, but because it removes one variable from a decision that already has too many. A firm that is uncertain about its trade exposure over the next two years does not need to also be uncertain about its real estate commitment over the next five.

Positioning For Either Direction

The practical implication for a firm weighing space decisions in this environment is not to avoid commitment altogether, but to match the length of the commitment to the length of the certainty actually available. A government-adjacent firm with steady, policy-driven demand may have every reason to commit to dedicated space now. A firm with meaningful trade exposure, or one whose near-term trajectory genuinely depends on how international negotiations unfold, has a reasonable case for holding that decision in reserve — while still maintaining the professional presence a credible firm needs in the meantime.

That is the case for treating a flexible arrangement not as a compromise, but as the correct instrument for a specific set of conditions. When those conditions clear, the move to a longer commitment remains available on the firm's own timeline, rather than one made under the pressure of trade conditions that have not yet settled.
